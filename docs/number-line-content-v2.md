## ACT II — ERA COPY, DEEPENED (replaces current era bodies; year markers unchanged unless noted)

### Era 1 · ~20,000 BC — The Tally
Before numbers, there were marks. The Ishango bone — a baboon fibula pulled from a
fishing settlement on the shore of Lake Edward by Jean de Heinzelin in 1950 — carries
three columns of deliberate tally notches, cut some twenty thousand years ago. An even
older bone from the Lebombo mountains carries twenty-nine. What the marks *meant* is
honestly contested: a count of things, a lunar calendar, or a mind simply playing with
quantity — the notch groupings on the Ishango bone include 11, 13, 17, 19, which has
tempted some to see primes and others to see coincidence. [disputed] What is not
contested: counting came first — one mark, one thing — and it came tens of thousands
of years before anyone wrote a numeral.
**Still missing:** numbers had no names, and no line to live on.
Citations: de Heinzelin, "Ishango," *Scientific American* (1962) · Marshack, *The Roots
of Civilization* (1972) — lunar reading, contested · Lebombo bone, Border Cave excavations.

### Era 2 · ~1800 BC — Babylon & Egypt
Babylonian scribes computed in base sixty on clay. The tablet YBC 7289 carries √2
correct to six sexagesimal digits — a millennium and a half before Pythagoras. The
tablet Plimpton 322 arranges fifteen rows of numbers that read, on one famous
interpretation, as a systematic table of Pythagorean triples; on a more recent reading,
as a teacher's list of reciprocal pairs. [disputed — both readings live] In Egypt, the
scribe Ahmes copied out the Rhind Papyrus around 1550 BC: arithmetic run entirely on
unit fractions. Numbers ran granaries, split inheritances, and predicted stars — and
were always a quantity *of something*.
**Still missing:** no one had drawn the numbers as places.
Citations: Neugebauer & Sachs, *Mathematical Cuneiform Texts* (1945) · Robson, "Neither
Sherlock Holmes nor Babylon," *Historia Mathematica* (2001) · YBC 7289, Yale Babylonian
Collection · Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, British Museum.

### Era 3 · ~500 BC — Greece
The Greeks made number geometric: lengths, magnitudes, ratios. Then came the scandal.
The diagonal of a square is incommensurable with its side — √2 fits on no ruler made of
whole-number marks. Legend blames the discovery on Hippasus of Metapontum and has the
Pythagoreans drowning him for it; the sources for the drowning are late and unreliable,
the mathematics is not. [attributed — the theorem is certain, the murder is legend]
Eudoxus rescued ratio with the theory of proportion that became Euclid's Book V, and
Book X classified the incommensurables — all without ever writing the number zero,
which Greek mathematics did not possess.
**Still missing:** nothing. Literally — the number zero did not exist.
Citations: Euclid, *Elements* V (Eudoxan proportion), X (incommensurables), XIII (the
pentagon and the extreme and mean ratio) · von Fritz, "The Discovery of
Incommensurability by Hippasus of Metapontum," *Annals of Mathematics* (1945).

### Era 4 · 628 AD — India
Brahmagupta, in the *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, did what no Greek had done: he treated
zero as a number in its own right and wrote down its arithmetic — and the arithmetic of
negatives, which he called debts, against fortunes. His rules are almost the modern
ones; where they crack (he ruled 0 ÷ 0 = 0) the crack is honest, and mathematics took
a thousand more years to mend it. The line gained a center and a left half. The system
travelled west through al-Khwārizmī's Baghdad, and Leonardo of Pisa — Fibonacci —
carried it into Europe in the *Liber Abaci* of 1202, where merchants adopted the
numerals long before philosophers accepted the debts.
**Still missing:** number and geometry still lived in separate worlds.
Citations: Brahmagupta, *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* ch. 18 (628) · al-Khwārizmī, *On the
Calculation with Hindu Numerals* (c. 820) · Fibonacci, *Liber Abaci* (1202).

### Era 5 · 1637 — Descartes, and the line actually drawn
*La Géométrie* — the appendix to the *Discourse on Method* — fused algebra with
geometry: a number is a position, an equation is a curve. Fermat reached coordinate
methods independently in the same years. But the number line itself, drawn as an
instrument, with negatives marching left of zero as honestly as positives march right,
is younger than most people guess: John Wallis put it on paper in his *Treatise of
Algebra* in 1685, arguing that a man who walks five yards forward and eight back
stands three yards behind where he began.
**Still missing:** the line still had gaps finer than any fraction.
Citations: Descartes, *La Géométrie* (1637) · Fermat, *Ad locos planos et solidos
isagoge* (c. 1636, publ. 1679) · Wallis, *A Treatise of Algebra* (1685) — the first
explicit negative number line.

### Era 6 · 1872 — Dedekind & Cantor
Dedekind's *Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen* filled every gap: each irrational number
is precisely a place where the rationals split — a cut. Two years later Cantor proved
the completed line holds uncountably many points ("Über eine Eigenschaft des Inbegriffes
aller reellen algebraischen Zahlen," 1874). The identification of the geometric line
with the real numbers — the Cantor–Dedekind axiom — closed a five-thousand-year
construction project.
**Still missing:** — (the existing full-width turn statement stays verbatim)
Citations: Dedekind, *Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen* (1872) · Cantor, *Crelles
Journal* 77 (1874).
